The Mystery of Time

Everything in quantum physics changes with time. Particles decay, atoms vibrate, and waves collapse. Time is everywhere in the equations, serving as the clock that makes quantum mechanics work. Yet, unlike matter made of atoms, or light made of photons, time is not made of anything.

In standard quantum mechanics, time is an external background—a stage where the drama of physics plays out. But in Einstein's General Relativity, time is a dynamic, physical fabric woven into space (spacetime) that bends and stretches around massive objects.

This paradox—that our most accurate atomic clocks can measure time to 18 decimal places without modern physics being able to fully explain what time is—remains the greatest unresolved problem in physics.

While the physics of time points to mysteries at the Planck scale, the flow of time leaves behind a physical record of humanity. Below, we trace how time has preserved the most studied collection of ancient texts in human history: The Bible. By mapping each book through time, authorship, and the physical manuscript evidence that survived the centuries, we see a remarkable timeline of preservation.

Conclusion: The Unfolding Plan of Eternity

In 525 AD, a monk named Dionysius Exiguus forever changed how we measure time. Tasked with calculating the date of Easter, he refused to use a calendar anchored to a Roman emperor who persecuted Christians. Instead, he reset the clock to the arrival of Jesus Christ, dividing all of earthly time into BC (Before Christ) and AD (Anno Domini, In the Year of Our Lord).

In modern times, there has been a push to replace these acronyms with BCE (Before Common Era) and CE (Common Era) to secularize the calendar. Yet, despite renaming the era, the profound reality remains: the entire world still numbers its days from the exact same origin point. We still use the timeline he originated, simply calling it something different.

It is a profound reminder of Ecclesiastes 3. While our lives are bound by inevitable seasons—"a time for war and a time for peace"—time itself is ultimately anchored by the One who stepped into our timeline, inviting us out of temporary struggles and into His eternal peace.

Invitation

When I worked for Lovell Custom Sheet Metal, John Panda was the foreman and boss of the composite shop, and I was his understudy. We worked inside the shop and were never in the field—except for me. I also did work in the field, and that’s where this story comes from.

In the shop, we prefabricated the entire building. Precision was everything. As the saying went, 'John Panda, we work in 64th of an inch in the composite shop, bud.' Being precise was key. If you were out by just 1/8 of an inch, by the time you were done with the wall of the building, the panels would not fit and would be out by feet.

So, I was out in the field, working on the James River Assembly wall that faces the highway. Because everything was prefab, the holes in the panels were all previously drilled. 'A' goes to 'B'—that kind of thing.

But out in the field, the structural framing we were attaching to wasn't perfectly level. Because of that, the panels were off. The workers assigned to the field were roughnecks, and they were getting all impatient. They're on this massive highway-facing wall, and they just wanted to get it done. They told me, 'Let's just drill new holes in the panels. It'll be fine. We already have the frame up.' They were rushing.

But because I had trained under John Panda, I was completely patient. I remembered the standard we needed. I told them, 'No, no. We want it done, but we want it done right.' I knew that if we started slipping by an eighth of an inch here and there, the whole wall would be a disaster by the end.

So I made them stop. We took the panels down. We went back to the framing, leveled it, re-checked it, re-RE-checked it, and did it the right way. And as we did that, because we went back and laid the right groundwork, everything just matched. The prefab holes lined up perfectly. Everything went well.

I look back on this and think, 'Man, that's how I approach life in so many ways.' I might have been the patient one on that James River Assembly wall, but in my own spiritual life? I'm often like those roughneck field workers. I just want to be done. And someone comes along and says, 'No, no, no. I know you're impatient, but you don't actually just want it to be done. You want it to be done right.'

I think there are maybe too many of us who go through life and regardless of the project we're working on, whatever the work is, we just want to be done with it. Why did those field workers want to be done building that wall? Because they wanted to go on to the next thing. And when they were doing that next thing, what would they want? They'd want to be done with that, too, so they could go on to the next thing.

So often, we go through life just wanting to be done with the work. The problem with that is, I'm then missing the work of the moment. In so many ways, if I just want to be done with it, when I miss the work of the moment, I'm also oftentimes missing the WORTH of the moment.

We will never ever encounter God in the future, we will never encounter God in the past. The only time we have to encounter God is in the present, in the moment. In this moment is the only time that I really have to either say yes to God or say no to God; to pay attention to God or to ignore God. Because this is the moment.

So often, though, I just want to be done with this moment. I want to be on to the next moment, because I'm always looking towards the future or I'm looking towards the past. And yet, the only moment any of us ever really have is right now. The present.

Let's go back to this: in so many parts of our lives, we just want to be done. I'll talk to people who will say, 'God, give me patience.' Why? Because they want to be done with the striving for patience. 'I want to have patience, so God, give me patience.' Or, 'I want to be the kind of person who is able to go through life without any fear. God, give me courage so I don't have to go through life afraid. I want to be DONE with being afraid.' Or, 'God, give me the ability to love people.' Why? Because we want to be done with the striving to become a person of love, or patience, or courage.

A lot of times in our lives, you recognize that there is a work that God is doing right now. And for a lot of us, we want Him to just be done with it. 'God, just do it. Let it be done.'

And God is like that standard we had in the composite shop. He says, 'No, no, no. We work in 64th of an inch, bud. We're doing it right now. We have to take it back to the foundation. We have to take it back a bunch of steps.'

We say, 'God, no, just be done.' And He says, 'No, we're going to do this right.'

The remarkable thing is this: God is so much more patient than we are, because He doesn't just want it done and over with. He doesn't just want the final result. He wants US. And the only moment that He gets to have us is right now. Because if we live the 'right now moment' in God's presence, that means we become the kind of people who can live the 'forever moment' in God's presence.

You realize that right now, the work of the moment is to become a person of love. Right now, the work of the moment is to become a patient person. Right now, the work of the moment is to become that generous person, or that fair person, or that person who is able to say, 'I don't just want to be done. I want it to be done right.'

And when we recognize that this is the only moment I have to do that work, then we begin to realize the worth of every single moment.
